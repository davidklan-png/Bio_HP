The spark

On 17 September I posted a question on LinkedIn: what's a problem outside your job you'd like to work on? I was looking for people who have spent a decade getting good at something, and whose skill now mostly goes into keeping the machine running.

Nathan Kemppainen, PMP, SPC, CSM, a PMO governance manager, answered. He wrote about how much single-use plastic packaging Japan throws away. He had toured a soy sauce factory and seen its soy byproducts, and he had spotted planters made from soy byproduct at a garden centre. With naphtha supply shocks and the long-term oil outlook, he asked how long it will be "before something like this becomes a viable alternative." I replied that I would love to investigate it with him.

The numbers made it worth doing. Japan produces about 700,000 tonnes of okara a year, the wet pulp left over from tofu and soymilk. Most of it is treated as industrial waste, and producers pay to dispose of it. Meanwhile naphtha, the feedstock for plastic packaging, hit a record price in 2026, and Calbee moved 14 products to black-and-white packaging during the shortage.

So the question became: could okara that Japan pays to throw away go into plastic packaging as a filler that costs less than nothing? Neither of us knew. Those planters, it turns out, are real: Sagamiya Foods sells pellets that are 51% okara in polypropylene, used for plant pots. The open question was whether it could work at scale.

The setup: one place to frame it, one to talk, one to keep the record

We split the work across three tools, each with one job. Nobody should need a special account or setup to follow along.

  • Kōsa frames the investigation: the brief, the open questions, the sharing boundary ("Any") and decisions.
  • Slack is where we talk: day-to-day discussion between Nate and me, and progress updates.
  • GitHub keeps the record: every source, evidence note, tool and diagram, reviewed through pull requests.
  • The dashboard shows progress: how well the evidence covers each question, plus the flow diagrams.

Kōsa turned a loose idea into a written brief with ten open questions. The first was the likely deal-breaker: okara is about 80% water, so does drying it cost more than the plastic it replaces? Others covered smell and colour, strength loss, recyclability, supply chains and prior art.

Slack went live on 27 September 2026. The GitHub repository and its first pull request followed two days later. From then on, we went from evaluating tagging and data structure to a fully functioning corpus with a dashboard to visualize the progress and shape the design.

AI is the basis of this work. Claude Code and GLM agents ran the searches, screened thousands of papers, read the full texts and drew the diagrams. Nate and I set the questions, the rules and the bar for evidence, and reviewed every result. The point is to close the gap between a human idea and a real-world picture of it: from a LinkedIn comment to a quantified map of 700,000 tonnes of okara in about a week.

Building the corpus: collect everything, then earn every fact

Before forming an opinion, we collected the evidence. In three days the corpus went from zero to 6,579 documents found, 808 kept and 463 evidence notes. AI agents did the searching, screening and reading; every decision rule was written down and every result was reviewed.

  1. Search. 134 logged queries in English and Japanese across CiNii, J-STAGE, Crossref and OpenAlex. Every run is recorded, so any search can be repeated.
  2. Screen. Each of the 6,579 results was checked against written criteria. 5,363 were excluded with a reason, 808 were kept, and 408 are still waiting.
  3. Read. Open-access full texts were fetched and read against the ten questions. Japanese scans and tables got the most careful reading, because those numbers feed the model.
  4. Extract. Each finding became an evidence note: the question, the evidence, the source, a confidence rating and a suggested next step. 463 notes now hold 938 numbers across 55 standard measures.
  5. Corroborate. Where sources disagree, both are kept and the newer or better one is marked as the one to use. Nothing is deleted.

The first payoff came quickly. Drying, the expected deal-breaker, is not one. At commercial scale it costs about ¥10–35 per kg of dry okara to run, roughly 6–11% of the value of the plastic it replaces. The real problem is scale: most tofu makers are too small, so drying has to be shared.

A public dashboard shows how well the evidence covers each question. Fifteen of the sixteen inputs to the cost model are now covered.

Answers as pictures: two Sankey diagrams

Our first question was simple: where does Japan's okara actually go? The answer fits in one picture, a Sankey diagram where each stream's width is its tonnage.

The two diagrams use the same scale and the same colours, so they can be compared at a glance. Red costs money, amber is sold cheap, green creates value. Hover over any number to see the evidence behind it.

The diagrams also changed the question. We started by asking whether okara could go into plastic. Now we ask which use of the burned half creates the most value. Packaging is one of five candidates, not the default answer.

What the corpus can answer next

The expensive part is done. Collecting, screening and structuring 6,579 documents is slow. Asking the next question of 808 structured sources is fast. Here are questions the corpus can take on now:

  • Which use of the burned half creates the most value per tonne? Composting, biogas, mushrooms or packaging, once fees, prices and carbon are weighed together.
  • Where should shared dryers go? Tofu plants per prefecture fell from 1,000–2,000 in 1964 to 37–260 in 2012. Supply is concentrating, which makes central drying easier to place.
  • How does okara compare with fillers already on the market? The corpus covers rice husk, tea leaves, coffee grounds and wood fibre in plastics, so okara's results can be checked against them.
  • What signals a plastics price squeeze early? Naphtha prices, PP price hikes and packaging changes like Calbee's.
  • Do okara-filled parts survive recycling? This is one of the thinnest areas of evidence, and one of the most important.
  • What are the product-safety risks? Soy allergens, rancidity, mould and shelf life.

Every new okara finding, from a dryer quote to a tofu maker's disposal bill, now has a place to land and a model to update.

The project is open. If you work in food processing, packaging, materials or waste, and you have a number, a source or a question, I'd like to hear from you. And my question from September still stands: what's a problem outside your job you'd like to work on?
