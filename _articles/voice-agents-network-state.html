1. Where We Are Now

In 2022, Balaji Srinivasan published The Network State, proposing that governance could start online, organized around shared values, and gradually coordinate in the physical world. Most people treated it as either science fiction or ideology. Some tried to build it.

Since then, the concept has moved from whitepaper to operational reality. The Network School launched on a private island near Singapore in September 2024, with enrollment growing from 150 to over 550 by mid-2025. A conference in October 2025 drew over 3,000 registrations and featured speakers including Vitalik Buterin, Brian Armstrong, and representatives from the governments of Singapore, Dubai, Abu Dhabi, and El Salvador. Additional campus locations are planned for Dubai, Tokyo, and Miami.

Meanwhile, Praxis, another high-profile network state project backed by Peter Thiel and others, secured $525 million in milestone-based financing and now claims over 150,000 registered citizens across 80 countries. Its proposed city, Atlas, is being planned at Vandenberg in California, with a parallel non-US site under consideration.

These are no longer theoretical exercises. They are active infrastructure projects with real capital behind them. That changes the stakes for anyone building the technical systems underneath.

Key Decision: Build vs. Wait

The network state ecosystem is moving fast but remains fragmented. Teams need to decide now whether to build governance tooling that can serve these emerging communities, or wait for standards to solidify. The risk of waiting: being locked out of early platform effects. The risk of building early: committing to architectures that don’t survive the first regulatory contact.

2. What the Network State Got Right

Think about how most people experience governance today. You live within institutions you did not design. You follow rules you had little role in shaping. Your vote, if you use it, is one among millions, aggregated into outcomes that rarely reflect your actual position.

Modern governance works at scale, but it does so by compressing people into abstractions: voters, taxpayers, demographics.

The Network State asked a different question: what if governance started from voluntary participation instead of inherited structure? Instead of trying to change a system you are embedded in, you could opt into one aligned with your values. If it works, people join. If it doesn’t, they leave.

This is exit-based governance, and it is not new. It shows up in markets, open-source communities, online platforms, and increasingly in DAOs. The core idea is powerful: you do not need universal agreement. You need enough alignment to make something viable.

3. Where It Falls Short

Exit has limits. Imagine 14 households on a street facing a six-month construction project. Each household has different constraints: work schedules, mobility needs, health concerns, family situations. One person needs midday access for a wheelchair. Another works night shifts and depends on overnight parking. Another needs quiet afternoons for medical reasons.

The Network State logic says: if the system does not work for you, leave, or build another one. But in many real-world contexts, you cannot. You cannot fork a street. You cannot fork a building. You cannot fork your physical environment.

Even in digital systems, constant exit leads to fragmentation. Communities split over disagreements that might have been resolvable, resulting in smaller groups that are internally aligned but externally limited. Noah Smith’s analysis ahead of the 2025 Network State Conference highlighted this directly, arguing that the original concept needed significant work to address public goods provision and physical infrastructure dependencies.

Exit enables choice. But by itself, it does not solve coordination. And coordination is where governance actually lives.

Key Decision: Exit Architecture

Any system you build must make exit cheap and data-portable. But it also needs to make staying worthwhile. If your coordination mechanisms are weak, people will fork constantly and you will never reach the density needed for meaningful governance. Design for both: clean exit paths AND high-fidelity internal representation.

4. The Missing Layer: Representation

The harder problem is not how communities form. It is how they function once people are already in them.

In the 14-household example, the challenge is not that people fundamentally disagree. It is that their needs are complex, interdependent, and difficult to reconcile through traditional mechanisms. Voting simplifies those needs into discrete options. Discussion is limited by time, attention, and social dynamics. Compromise often means losing important constraints along the way.

But from a computational perspective, this is not unusual. It is a coordination problem with multiple constraints, the kind that optimization systems already handle in domains like logistics, scheduling, and resource allocation.

The issue is not that a solution does not exist. It is that the system we use to find it cannot represent enough detail.

5. What Voice Agents Change

A voice agent is not a chatbot or a voting proxy. It is a system that can represent your preferences, constraints, and priorities with far more fidelity than a ballot or a survey. Instead of asking you to reduce your position to a single choice, it carries a structured version of your perspective into a shared decision process.

Now imagine each of the 14 households has such an agent. Instead of voting or debating in a limited time window, their agents can represent detailed constraints such as access times, noise sensitivity, and mobility needs. They can explore possible solutions across those constraints, identify options that satisfy the maximum number of requirements, and surface true conflicts where needs are genuinely incompatible.

The goal is not to decide for you. It is to make your position legible and actionable in a system that can actually work with it. In many cases, that reveals solutions that would never emerge from a two-hour meeting.

5.1 What Has Changed Since the Original Article

The landscape around agent-mediated governance has shifted substantially since this concept was first proposed. Three developments matter most for anyone building these systems:

  • DAO-AI is now empirically validated. In October 2025, researchers from Columbia University and IBM published DAO-AI, the first empirical study of agentic AI as autonomous decision-makers in decentralized governance. Using over 3,000 proposals from major protocols, they built an agentic voter that interprets proposal contexts, retrieves historical deliberation data, and independently determines its voting position. Crucially, they found strong alignment between agent decisions and human-weighted outcomes. This is not theoretical anymore; there is measurable evidence that agents can approximate collective reasoning at scale.
  • Multi-agent governance frameworks are emerging. The World Economic Forum published its AI Agents in Action framework in late 2025, specifically addressing evaluation and governance for autonomous agents. Singapore’s IMDA released the world’s first governance framework specifically for agentic AI in January 2026, introducing concepts like Agent Identity Cards that specify capabilities, limitations, and escalation protocols. These are not abstract guidelines; they are the regulatory floor your system will need to meet.
  • Real DAOs are already deploying AI governance tools. Major platforms like MakerDAO and NEAR are integrating AI-powered proposal summarizers and autonomous agents into their governance pipelines. Aave has deployed tools that help members process governance discussions in minutes instead of hours. By late 2025, close to a third of major DAO decisions were either guided or partially executed by AI systems. The pattern is clear: agent-mediated governance is not a future concept; it is a current deployment reality.

Key Decision: Agent Fidelity vs. Autonomy

The biggest architectural choice you face is how much authority agents carry. A high-fidelity agent that only represents your preferences and surfaces options is a fundamentally different system from one that votes on your behalf. The DAO-AI research shows agents can align well with human decisions, but the governance and liability implications are worlds apart. Start with representation. Only move to delegation when you have auditable alignment data and a clear escalation protocol.

6. Inside vs. Outside Governance

The Network State and voice agents address different parts of the same problem. The Network State focuses on how communities form: who joins, what values define the group, whether participation is voluntary. This is governance at the boundary, what you might call the outside.

Voice agents focus on how communities operate internally: how decisions are made, how conflicts are resolved, how shared resources are allocated. This is governance on the inside.

You need both. Without exit, governance risks becoming rigid or unaccountable. Without representation, governance becomes shallow, responsive in theory, but not in practice.

Exit provides legitimacy. Representation provides resolution.

7. What This Could Look Like

Consider a community of a few hundred people: a professional network, a DAO, a neighborhood group, a co-living space. Each member has a voice agent that evolves through interaction, capturing preferences, clarifying priorities, and learning where flexibility exists and where it does not.

When a decision arises, such as budgeting, scheduling, or membership criteria, agents participate in the process. They do not replace humans. They extend them. They explore the solution space more thoroughly than a group discussion can, make tradeoffs explicit, and surface when a decision impacts specific individuals in ways that would otherwise be invisible.

When necessary, especially around value conflicts, decisions are escalated back to people. And if the system stops working for you, you leave. That is still essential. But ideally, you do not need to leave as often, because your position is actually being accounted for.

8. The Regulatory Reality You Cannot Ignore

Any system builder needs to understand the regulatory ground is shifting fast. The EU AI Act, adopted in 2024, classifies AI systems into risk tiers but was negotiated before the explosion of agentic AI. Its risk categories assume AI systems that assist human decision-making, not systems that make and execute decisions independently. This creates a governance gap for agent-mediated governance systems.

Singapore has moved faster. Its January 2026 framework for agentic AI introduces Agent Identity Cards, a standardized disclosure format specifying what an agent can do, what it cannot, where it is authorized to act, and how it escalates. This is directly relevant to voice agent architectures.

In the United States, AI governance remains sectoral: the SEC has proposed rules for AI-driven investment recommendations, the FTC has taken enforcement actions on deceptive AI claims, and NIST launched its AI Agent Standards Initiative in January 2026. There is no unified federal framework, which means your system will face different compliance requirements depending on domain and jurisdiction.

Key Decision: Compliance Architecture

Build auditability into the core, not as an afterthought. Every agent action, every preference it represents, every decision it participates in must be logged, traceable, and explainable. Singapore’s Agent Identity Card concept is a good minimum model. If you cannot explain what an agent did and why at any point in time, you will not survive regulatory scrutiny in any jurisdiction that matters.

9. Why This Matters Now

We are entering a world where agents will increasingly participate in decision-making systems: negotiating, allocating, coordinating. Gartner predicts 40% of enterprise applications will integrate AI agents by the end of 2026, up from under 5% in 2025. That is not a gradual adoption curve; it is a vertical line.

The question is not whether this happens. It is who those agents represent. If they represent institutions, platforms, or optimization goals detached from individuals, governance becomes even more abstract. If they represent people, with fidelity and accountability, governance could become more precise than it has ever been.

The Network State introduced a model for voluntary participation. Voice agents introduce a model for meaningful representation. Together, they point toward something new: systems where participation is chosen, and representation is granular enough to matter.

10. Key Risks and Open Questions

10.1 Alignment Drift

An agent that faithfully represents you today may drift as your preferences evolve and the model does not keep up. The DAO-AI research evaluated alignment at a point in time; longitudinal alignment over months or years is untested. You need continuous calibration mechanisms, not just initial setup.

10.2 Attack Surface

Multi-agent systems introduce cascading permission chains and emergent behaviors that single-agent governance models cannot address. A compromised agent in a multi-agent system can potentially influence or instruct all agents it coordinates with. Zero-trust architecture is not optional here.

10.3 Power Concentration

DAO governance already exhibits persistent power concentration, with fewer than 10% of token holders typically participating in votes. AI agents could either democratize participation by lowering barriers, or they could amplify concentration if sophisticated agents are only available to well-resourced participants. Your pricing and access model is itself a governance decision.

10.4 The Legitimacy Question

If an agent votes differently than you would have in a specific case, but aligns with your stated preferences, is that legitimate representation? This is not a technical question. It is a political one, and your community will need to answer it before deployment, not after.

Key Decision: Failure Mode Design

Every system fails. The question is how. Design for graceful degradation: if the agent system goes down, governance must still function through traditional mechanisms. If an agent is compromised, the blast radius must be contained. If alignment drifts, there must be a human-in-the-loop circuit breaker. These are not edge cases; they are your primary design constraints.

11. The Open Question

If you could join a system where your perspective was represented with real fidelity, not reduced to a vote, not averaged into a demographic, would you? And if you could leave at any time, would you still need to?

That is not a philosophical question. It is an empirical one. We can test it in small communities. We can measure outcomes. We can learn where it breaks.

The tools are emerging. The constraints are clear. What remains uncertain is whether we are willing to rethink governance at the level where it actually happens: not just how we enter systems, but how we live inside them.
