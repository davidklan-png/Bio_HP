LinkedIn told me to "celebrate her with a post or video."

So I stopped and actually thought about it. Not the corporate version of "thinking about it" where you pick a safe answer and tag someone. I mean I sat with the question: which women shaped who I am?

And the answer broke LinkedIn's whole premise.

My mom. She raised me. She showed me what relentless looks like long before I ever heard the word "resilience" in a leadership seminar. She didn't have a professional brand. She had a family that needed to eat and a will that refused to quit. Everything I know about pushing through when things fall apart, I learned watching her do it without applause.

My sisters. They taught me how to listen — mostly by making it very clear when I wasn't. They taught me that being tough and being kind aren't opposites. That women don't exist in the neat categories the world tries to put them in. They shaped how I treat every woman I work with, and they did it around a kitchen table, not in a boardroom.

My teachers. The ones who saw something in a kid before he saw it in himself. Who stayed late, who pushed harder, who called me out when I was being pernicious. Women who built the foundation of every professional skill I have — and who will never see a LinkedIn notification about it.

None of these women are on LinkedIn. Not one.

And that's what hit me: LinkedIn's version of "celebrate her" only works if she's in the network.

The platform asked me to honor the women who shaped my life, but only within a system designed to convert that honor into engagement. My mom doesn't have a profile to tag. My sisters aren't going to see a "thoughtful post" between job listings and hustle culture. My teachers retired into lives that have nothing to do with content algorithms.

So who exactly is the post for?

Here's where it gets uncomfortable:

The women who influenced me most did it invisibly. No audience. No metrics. No personal brand strategy. They just showed up — relentlessly, thanklessly — and did the work of shaping a human being.

And now a platform wants me to repay that by... generating content?

That's not celebration. That's someone else's engagement strategy wearing gratitude as a costume, virtue signaling.

The real women who changed my life don't need a post. They need a phone call. A visit. A son, a brother, a former student who actually became something because of what they gave.

That's the ROI they were after. And it doesn't come with a like button.

So no, I didn't post about International Women's Day.

I called my mom instead. We talked for an hour. She told me a story I'd never heard about some FBI agents she and her friend went on a date with before she met my dad. I laughed. She reluctantly held back any other details. I told her I shared her mind and her personality and I love this life. And for the first time in a long time, it felt like I'd actually said something that mattered.

Then I texted my sisters. Then I turned to my wife and said, "Thanks for putting up with my shit."

And here's the thing: it felt incredible. Thank you LinkedIn for this inspiration!

Not the hollow dopamine of a notification. Not the performance anxiety of wondering if the post "landed." Just the clean, undeniable feeling of a real human connection with someone who changed your life — and finally telling them so.

So here's my challenge to you:

Close LinkedIn for 12 minutes. Meditate on the women who actually shaped you. Not the ones who'd make the best content — the ones who made you.

Now pick up your phone. Not to post. To call.

Send the text that's just bubbled up after a moment of quiet reflection. Write the email you've been meaning to write. Show up at her door with coffee and say the thing you should've said a long time ago.

I promise you — that moment will mean more to her than a thousand tagged posts ever could. And it'll mean more to you too. And don't mention LinkedIn.
